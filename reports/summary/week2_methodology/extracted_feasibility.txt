1. Executive summary and headline recommendation
This document reviews the candidate methodologies for the Topic C1 benchmark, judged against three things at once: what the recent literature says works, what actually won comparable competitions, and what one person can genuinely build and evaluate honestly in two to three weeks. The conclusions below are opinionated on purpose — a neutral list of every possible model would not help anyone decide.
1.1 The five findings that should drive the plan
The item catalogue is 100 clusters, not 100,000 hotels. This single fact invalidates most of the current sequential-recommendation and generative-recommendation literature as a source of technique for us. Semantic IDs, approximate nearest-neighbour retrieval, sampled softmax, two-tower retrieval, and the whole "scaling to large catalogues" research line exist to solve a problem we do not have. Full ranking over 100 items is exact, cheap, and fits in memory — which is a gift for evaluation rigour, and a warning that "state of the art" papers are optimising for a different regime.
Our own destination-conditioned popularity baseline already reaches Recall@5 = 53.07%. Published academic work on this same dataset reports MAP@5 in the 0.49–0.50 range using XGBoost and Random Forest ensembles [13][14]. The metrics are not identical, but they are close enough in spirit that the comparison must be made in the report: a one-line frequency count conditioned on srch_destination_id is competitive with published machine-learning results on Expedia. Any model family we evaluate must be measured against that bar, not against the 14.06% global-popularity bar, or the benchmark will flatter every model in the table.
The median booking user has 2 bookings. Sequence models are in the mentor's plan and must be run, but we should expect them to be close to, not far above, a well-tuned context-aware baseline, because there is very little sequence to learn from at the user level. Recent work makes exactly this point: whether a dataset has enough sequential structure to justify sequential models is itself a measurable property, not an assumption [11]. Treating a weak sequence-model result as an honest negative finding — and measuring why — is a stronger RQ1 answer than forcing the model to look good.
The strongest realistic model here is a context-aware feature model, not a neural sequence model. The target is one of 100 classes and every row carries rich trip context (party composition, dates, package flag, destination, distance, channel, device). That is a supervised tabular ranking problem, and the evidence from RecSys Challenge 2024 (LightGBM Ranker with lambdarank won the news-recommendation track [15]) and from the wider tabular literature is that gradient-boosted trees remain extremely hard to beat in this regime [16].
LLM reranking is the highest-risk component and needs to be redesigned before it is built. hotel_cluster is an anonymous integer. There is no item text for an LLM to reason about, which is the assumption behind essentially every LLM-ranking paper including LLMRank [7]. Either we synthesise a textual profile per cluster from training statistics and let the LLM reason over that, or the component has no honest input. This is discussed in detail in §10, including the cost and latency envelope.
1.2 Recommended plan in one paragraph
Build the evaluation harness first and make the destination-conditioned popularity baseline the reference line, not global popularity. Then implement, in this order: neighbourhood and linear baselines (ItemKNN, EASE), matrix factorisation (iALS and BPR via an established library), item2vec, a gradient-boosted context-aware ranker as the "strong tabular" entry, then GRU4Rec and SASRec via RecBole, then an LLM reranker on a deliberately subsampled test slice with cost and latency logged. If time runs short, cut the second sequence model (SASRec or GRU4Rec, keep one), and cut the LLM reranker to a qualitative case study on a few hundred requests rather than a metric-bearing table row — but do not cut the baselines or the ablation, because those are what answer RQ1 and RQ4.
1.3 Shortlist at a glance
Applying feasibility and relevance to this data regime, and discarding the lines of work that are obsolete or mis-sized for a 100-item catalogue, the benchmark narrows to four learned methodologies on top of the mandatory baseline floor: (1) a context-aware gradient-boosted ranker, (2) a GRU4Rec sequence model run on two sequence definitions, (3) matrix factorisation as the traditional-CF reference, and (4) LLM reranking over synthesised cluster profiles on a subsample. The ranking, the demoted alternatives and the discarded lines are set out in §16; the Expedia deep-dive that drives the ranking is §3.
2. What this problem actually is (and why the label "RecSys" is misleading here)
2.1 Task definition as we will implement it
Given a user and the full trip context of a search event (party composition, check-in and check-out dates, destination searched, package flag, channel, device, user location, distance to destination), rank the 100 hotel clusters so that the cluster actually booked appears as high as possible. Training signal is the booking event (is_booking == 1); clicks are a separate, weaker event type and are never used as positive ground truth.

The cluster distribution is remarkably flat for a recommendation catalogue. There is no item cold-start at all — every cluster has at least 2,465 training bookings — and no dominant head. Two consequences follow. First, the popularity-bias metric that normally separates recommenders on long-tail catalogues will be compressed here; intra-list diversity and per-destination coverage are the beyond-accuracy metrics that will actually discriminate between models (RQ4). Three implications for method design:
srch_destination_id has 59,455 levels and a heavy tail of singletons. It cannot be one-hot encoded and a tree learner with native categorical support will overfit the singletons. It needs a learned embedding (in neural and FM models), or frequency/target encoding with a minimum-count threshold and explicit back-off to hotel_market (in tree models). hotel_market, with 2,118 levels and 99.998% test coverage, is the natural back-off key.
Counts are thin exactly where it matters. The 72.27% of bookings in the top-1,000 destinations are well served by counting; the remaining 27.73% are where a model that shares statistical strength across destinations — through market, country, continent, or the 149 latent dimensions of destinations.csv — should beat a frequency table. That is the measurable gap for the learned models to close.
Within-destination accuracy will be bimodal: near-deterministic destinations (cluster 54 at 72.2%) alongside diffuse ones. Report per-destination Recall@K as a distribution and slice by destination volume, not as a single mean.

Insight — The is_package flag is the one low-cardinality context field with a genuinely different cluster preference (top cluster 65 versus 91), and its share declines steadily from 21.78% to 9.42% across the two years. It is both a strong feature and a source of train/test shift: the test window has the lowest package share in the whole series. Any model that leans on is_package will face a different mix at test time than in training. Report package-slice accuracy separately and note the drift.

Consequences for the protocol and for the methods:
The popularity tables in §2.3 were built on all of train, which over-weights 2013 relative to the test period. A recency-weighted popularity variant (exponential decay on booking date) is a one-line extension that should be added as a baseline row, because if it beats the unweighted table, every learned model needs a recency feature too.
Validation should be the quarter immediately before test (July–September 2014), not a random slice of train. On a growth curve this steep, a random validation set is not representative of what the test set looks like.
The test window contains only one booking-date season (Q4), so a "season" slice on test must be by check-in month, which spreads across all twelve months and into 2015. Slicing test by booking month is not possible under this split and should not be attempted.
The falling conversion rate means the click-to-booking ratio in test (≈ 12:1 or worse) differs from early train (≈ 10:1). Any model trained on click-augmented sequences inherits that drift; the click-augmented rows must be evaluated with this caveat stated.
3.5 Weekly and daily rhythm: usable, but low priority
Booking volume has a strong weekly cycle (weekday factor Monday 1.136 to Saturday 0.728; weekend share 22.36%; lag-7 autocorrelation 0.975) and a clean daily profile (peak hour 11:00 with 195,819 bookings). The series has 27 anomalous days out of 724, 14 of them on US public holidays, and no unexplained anomalies in the booking series itself. Weekday and hour of booking are cheap features, but everything measured so far says they move volume, not cluster choice. Include them in the gradient-boosted model because they cost nothing; do not expect them to matter, and do not build anything around them.

Two things stand out. First, lead time, stay length, party composition and the package flag together form a "trip archetype" — a one-night solo booking made nine days ahead is a different product from a family week booked three weeks out — and that archetype is something a hospitality client can recognise and act on. A model that exposes it (a gradient-boosted model with these features, or an FM with their interactions) produces findings that translate to the Vinpearl context; a pure collaborative model does not. Second, booking rows need almost no cleaning, whereas click rows contain implausible dates and lead times and must be range-filtered before any session-based experiment. The missingness of orig_destination_distance (33.83%) is itself informative and should be kept as an indicator rather than imputed away.
3.8 Clicks and sessions: the hidden feasibility caveat
There are 34,669,600 click rows against 3,000,693 bookings — 11.55 clicks per booking, with a mean daily conversion of 8.38%. That volume is what makes the session-based variant of the sequence models attractive. But the dataset has no session identifier. Sessions must be inferred from user_id and date_time using an inactivity gap, and the choice of gap changes sequence lengths and therefore results. The Kaggle cnt field (up to 104 distinct values) further means a single click row may represent several aggregated events. Both decisions must be fixed in docs/eval_protocol.md before the session pipeline is built, and both are additional reasons to treat the session-based results as a separate experiment with its own caveats rather than as a drop-in comparison to the booking-history results.

4. Why the Expedia Kaggle leaderboard is not a methodology reference
The mentor asked for top-performing methods on Kaggle. For this specific dataset, that instruction has to be handled carefully, because the Expedia Hotel Recommendations competition was decided by a data leak rather than by modelling quality.
The leak works as follows: rows in the test set can be matched back to rows in the training set on a composite key of user_location_country, user_location_region, user_location_city, hotel_market and orig_destination_distance. Because orig_destination_distance is a near-unique continuous value for a given user–destination pair, that match effectively re-identifies the same search. Public write-ups of the competition report that roughly one third of the test set could be recovered this way with very high accuracy, and the leading solutions layered conventional models (Random Forest, XGBoost, SGDClassifier, BernoulliNB, blended by weighted probability averaging) on top of the leak-matched predictions to fill in the remainder [1][2].
Consequence
What we do about it
Public leaderboard scores are not comparable to an honest offline benchmark.
Never cite Kaggle leaderboard positions as a performance target in the final report. Cite peer-reviewed or reproducible results instead.
orig_destination_distance is a leakage-adjacent feature.
Treat it as a legitimate context feature (it is genuine trip information: how far the traveller is from the destination) but never construct train/test matching on the composite key above. Document the decision in docs/eval_protocol.md.
The competition protocol used a chronological public/private split of its own.
Irrelevant to us — we define our own temporal split. Do not inherit the competition's split or its MAP@5 metric uncritically.
Published academic MAP@5 figures on this dataset (≈0.4885 XGBoost, ≈0.496 ensemble [13][14]) may or may not include leak-adjacent features.
Quote them as context, with an explicit caveat that we could not verify whether the leak was excluded. Do not treat them as a target to beat.
Insight — This is a genuine contribution the report can make. Most student-level work on Expedia either reproduces the leak without realising it or quotes leaderboard numbers as if they were model quality. A benchmark that states the leak, excludes it by construction, and reports honest numbers against a destination-conditioned baseline is more defensible than one with better-looking metrics. Say so explicitly in the methodology section — examiners reward that.
5. Method family 1 — Non-personalised and neighbourhood baselines
Required by CLAUDE.md §8 and by every credible benchmark protocol. In this problem they are not a formality: as §2.3 shows, one of them is a genuinely strong model.
5.1 Members of this family
Method
Mechanism
Why it belongs in this benchmark
Global popularity
Rank clusters by training-set booking frequency.
The floor. Measured: 14.06% Recall@5.
Context-conditioned popularity
Frequency table keyed by a context value, with back-off to global when the key is unseen.
The real reference line. Measured: 53.07% Recall@5 on destination. Must be the comparison point for every learned model.
Repeat-last-cluster
Predict the user's most recent booked cluster first, then back off to popularity.
Tests how much of the signal is pure repetition. Measured: 16.8%. Low, which tells us travellers do not re-book the same cluster often — relevant to RQ4 and to the exploitation/exploration framing.
ItemKNN
Cosine or conditional-probability similarity between clusters from co-occurrence in user histories; score by similarity to the user's history.
The classic baseline that the reproducibility literature repeatedly shows beating neural models [8][10]. Trivial to compute on a 100×100 similarity matrix.
Session-based kNN (VSKNN / SKNN)
Nearest-neighbour over sessions with position and recency weighting.
Only applicable if we define a session unit (click-then-book within a search session). Ludewig and Jannach show VSKNN outperforming GRU4Rec on a majority of datasets tested [10].
EASE (Embarrassingly Shallow Autoencoder)
Closed-form ridge-regression item–item model with a zero-diagonal constraint; a single matrix inversion.
Its known weakness is scaling to large item sets — irrelevant here, because inverting a 100×100 matrix is instantaneous. For us EASE is essentially free and is a very strong linear collaborative-filtering baseline [5][6].
5.2 Pros
Cheap: all of these run on CPU in minutes, including on the full booking table.
Transparent: every prediction can be traced to a count or a similarity, which makes debugging the evaluation harness far easier than debugging it against a neural model.
They de-risk the whole project: if the harness is wrong, these surface it immediately, because their expected behaviour is known.
EASE in particular is the rare case of a method that is both simple and genuinely competitive; the literature has repeatedly found linear shallow autoencoders hard to beat on implicit feedback [5][6].
5.3 Cons
No context generalisation. Context-conditioned popularity fragments exactly as measured in §2.3 (destination × month is worse than destination alone).
Cold users get only the fallback. With 30.14% of test users unseen in train, the personalised members of this family (ItemKNN, EASE) degrade to popularity for nearly a third of the test set.
No mechanism to use rich numeric context (distance, dates, party size) at all.
Personal insight — Budget a full day for the popularity family alone, and build it as a configurable back-off chain rather than six separate scripts: a list of key columns, tried in order of specificity, falling back when a key is unseen or has too few observations. That one component gives you global popularity, per-destination, per-market, per-destination×package and any future combination as configuration rather than code, and it doubles as the cold-start fallback for every other model in the benchmark. It is the highest-leverage two hundred lines in the project.

6. Method family 2 — Matrix factorisation and latent-factor collaborative filtering

Fair comparison here is not a formality; it is the difference between a valid and an invalid benchmark table.

7. Method family 3 — item2vec and embedding-based item similarity
item2vec applies the skip-gram-with-negative-sampling objective to sequences of items instead of sequences of words, producing item embeddings whose geometry reflects co-occurrence in user histories. In the mentor's plan it sits between MF and the sequence models.
7.1 How it would be applied here
Sequence unit option A: the user's booked clusters ordered by date_time. Problem: median length 2, so most "sequences" are a single pair.
Sequence unit option B: clusters appearing within a single search session (clicks plus the booking), ordered by time. This produces far longer sequences and is where the co-occurrence signal actually lives, but it mixes a weak signal (clicks) into the training data, so it must be labelled as such and run as a separate configuration.
Scoring: average the embeddings of the user's history and rank clusters by cosine similarity, with the usual popularity back-off for cold users.
7.2 Pros
Cheap and fast: gensim Word2Vec on a few million short sequences trains in minutes on CPU.
It is the natural place to use the click data legitimately, which turns the 92%-clicks problem from a liability into an experiment (RQ1: does the weak signal help?).
Produces a genuinely good figure: a 2-D projection of 100 cluster embeddings is legible in a way that a 100,000-item embedding map never is. Small catalogue, real advantage.
7.3 Cons
With 100 items, the embedding space is tiny and the method is close to a smoothed co-occurrence matrix — it may not be distinguishable from ItemKNN, and if the two land within noise of each other that should be reported rather than hidden.
No context awareness, same structural handicap as MF.
The literature is a decade old; there is no recent paper to cite for method improvements, only for its role as a baseline.
Personal insight — Run item2vec on the click-augmented sessions rather than on the sparse booking sequences. On booking sequences it will be a slightly worse ItemKNN and will contribute nothing to the report. On click-then-book sessions it answers a question nobody else in the benchmark answers: whether the 92% of rows we are otherwise discarding contain usable structure. Frame it that way in the results table — "item2vec (bookings)" and "item2vec (click-augmented)" as two rows — and the family earns its place.

8. Method family 4 — Context-aware feature models (FM/DeepFM) and gradient-boosted rankers
This family is not named explicitly in the five-family plan, but it is where the mentor's Phase-4 "context-aware ablation" actually lives, and on the evidence it is the family most likely to produce the best number in the benchmark. It deserves to be a first-class entry, not an appendix.
8.1 Two sub-families
8.1.1 Factorisation Machines and their neural descendants
FM models pairwise interactions between all features through factorised weights, which makes it well-suited to sparse categorical context. DeepFM adds an MLP branch sharing the same embeddings, removing the need for manual cross features. The family is well covered in RecBole's context-aware category, so running it costs configuration rather than code. The honest caveat is that recent benchmarking work has not established a decisive advantage for the deep variants over well-tuned FM on typical tabular context [17], so we should run FM first and treat DeepFM as an optional extension.
8.1.2 Gradient-boosted decision trees
Two framings are available, and they are not equivalent:
Multiclass classification: one model, 100 classes, softmax over clusters. Simple, directly produces a ranking, and matches how the published Expedia work was done [13][14].
Learning-to-rank (LambdaMART / lambdarank objective): expand each search event into 100 candidate rows (one per cluster) with context and cluster-level features, and train a ranker with the booked cluster as the relevant item. This is the framing that won the RecSys Challenge 2024 news track with LightGBM Ranker [15], and it lets us add cluster-side features (historical popularity in this destination, seasonal profile) that the multiclass framing cannot express.
8.2 Pros
Uses every context field directly — party size, children count, room count, check-in month, lead time, length of stay, package flag, channel, device, distance — with no manual embedding design.
Handles the 30.14% cold users natively: a user with no history is simply a row with missing user-history features, and the tree splits on context instead. No fallback policy needed, which is a substantial practical advantage over MF and the sequence models.
The feature-group ablation the mentor asks for in Phase 4 is structurally trivial here: retrain with a feature group dropped, compare. The same ablation on a neural model requires architecture changes.
Strong prior evidence: gradient boosting continues to win applied ranking competitions and remains the reference on tabular data [15][16].
Feature importance and SHAP give the report interpretable figures answering "which context matters", which is literally RQ2's question form applied to travel.
8.3 Cons
The lambdarank framing multiplies the training table by 100 (3M events → 300M candidate rows). This is the one place in the project with a real memory and time problem, and it must be managed by sampling events (not rows within an event) or by training on a stratified subsample.
It is not a "recommender" in the collaborative-filtering sense, and a naive reader may object that it is "just classification". The rebuttal is that this is exactly what the published work on this dataset does, and what industry ranking stacks do — but it needs to be argued in the report rather than assumed.
Little novelty. Nobody will be impressed by LightGBM. The contribution has to come from the ablation and the comparison, not the model.
Personal insight — There is a real risk that this family wins the benchmark and thereby "spoils" the narrative that sequence models and LLMs are the interesting part. Plan for that outcome instead of fearing it: if a context-aware GBDT beats GRU4Rec, SASRec and the LLM reranker on sparse two-booking travel data, that is a clean, defensible and genuinely useful answer to RQ1 — "on this data regime, context beats sequence, and here is the measured margin". A benchmark whose conclusion is "the simplest strong method wins" is a good benchmark. A benchmark that quietly under-tunes the simple method to protect the narrative is misconduct.
Personal insight — Build the multiclass LightGBM first — one day, no candidate expansion, immediate strong number. Only invest in the lambdarank candidate-expansion framing if there is time left in week three. The marginal gain is real but the engineering cost is the largest in the plan.

9. Method family 5 — Sequential and session-based neural models
GRU4Rec and SASRec are explicitly required by the phase plan, and they are the arm of the benchmark most exposed to the dataset's structure. This section is longer than the others because the risk of producing a misleading result here is highest.

Framework implementation quality varies; the same model can score differently across libraries [3]. Our results are therefore statements about a specific implementation, and the report must say so.

9.6 The closest industrial analogue: Booking.com WSDM WebTour 2021
This is the most directly transferable competition result available, and it deserves study before the sequence models are built. The task was to predict the final city of a multi-destination trip from the preceding bookings in that trip — structurally the same shape as our problem, on anonymised city identifiers, with trip context (check-in/check-out, booker country, device, affiliate channel) on over a million reservations [18][19].
The winning solution, from an NVIDIA team, combined three architectures — MLP with a session-based matrix factorisation head (MLP-SMF), GRU with multi-stage SMF (GRU-MS-SMF), and XLNet with SMF — and ensembled them, reporting a leaderboard precision@4 of 0.5939, about 2.8% ahead of second place. Two techniques improved every architecture: the session-based matrix factorisation head, which learns a linear map between item embeddings and trip embeddings and scores by dot product, and data augmentation by reversing trip sequences [19][20].
Personal insight — Two things here are cheap for us to borrow and are worth more than any exotic architecture. First, the SMF head: scoring by dot product between a learned trip representation and an item embedding table is a small change to any model we build and was reported to help across all three of their architectures. Second, sequence reversal as augmentation: with a median history of two bookings, augmentation is exactly what our sparse sequences need, and reversal is a two-line transformation. Neither requires adopting their stack. Note honestly that reversal assumes trip order is partly symmetric, which is more plausible for a multi-city trip than for our cross-trip booking history — so test it as an ablation rather than assuming it transfers.
10. Method family 6 — LLM-based reranking
The most fashionable component of the plan and the one most likely to disappoint if built naively. This section sets out what the recent literature actually reports and what design survives contact with our data.
10.1 The state of the evidence
LLMRank (ECIR 2024) is the canonical reference implementation: it formats a user's interaction history and a candidate list into an instruction and has the LLM output a ranking. It is built on RecBole, so it slots into the same stack we are already using. Its headline findings are that LLMs have promising zero-shot ranking ability but suffer from position bias and popularity bias, which can be partly mitigated by bootstrapping (shuffling candidate order across repeated calls) and recency-focused prompting [7].
Cold-start reranker diagnostics (2026) report concrete failure modes for LLM and cross-encoder rerankers in cold-start settings: low retrieval coverage, severe exposure bias, and minimal score discrimination between relevant and irrelevant items. They also quantify the cost: a Qwen3-8B reranker at 58.7 pairs per second and 36 GB of memory, roughly three orders of magnitude slower than a collaborative-filtering retriever and about nine times more memory-hungry [21].
The 2025 cold-start survey frames LLMs as most valuable exactly where collaborative signal is absent — which, given our 30.14% unseen-user test slice, is the strongest argument for including them at all [22].
10.2 The blocking problem, and the design that solves it
Every one of those papers assumes items have text. Ours do not: hotel_cluster is an integer between 0 and 99 with no description, no name, and no attributes. Passing "rank clusters 12, 47, 63, 8, 91" to an LLM is not a meaningful experiment — the model has nothing to reason over, and any result would be noise or memorised bias.
The design that makes this honest is to synthesise a textual profile for each of the 100 clusters from training data only, and rerank over those profiles:
For each cluster, compute from the training split: its top destinations and markets, its check-in month distribution, its party-composition mix (solo/couple/family/group), its package share, its typical distance band, and its overall booking share.
Render those statistics into a short natural-language profile per cluster (three to five sentences), generated by a deterministic template, not by an LLM, so the profile is reproducible and auditable.
At inference, give the LLM the user's trip context in natural language, the candidate top-N clusters from the best first-stage model with their profiles, and ask for a reranking with a one-sentence justification.
Evaluate the reranked top-N against the same metrics as every other model, on a deliberately subsampled test slice, with cost and latency logged per request as CLAUDE.md §8 requires.
Personal insight — This cluster-profile design is the one genuinely novel contribution available in this project. It converts an anonymous ID benchmark into a text-reasoning benchmark without inventing data, because every sentence in a profile traces back to a training-set statistic. It also gives a clean ablation nobody else on this dataset has run: profiles with full statistics versus profiles with the destination statistics removed versus no profile at all (bare IDs). If the bare-ID condition scores at chance and the full-profile condition beats the first stage, that is a clean demonstration that the LLM is reasoning over the supplied context rather than recalling anything about Expedia. Build that three-way ablation deliberately — it is the difference between a demo and a result.
Personal insight — Be ruthless about scale. Reranking the full 600,139-row test set is not merely expensive, it is pointless: the metric would be dominated by first-stage retrieval quality and the LLM contribution would be invisible. Rerank a stratified subsample — a few thousand events, stratified across cold and warm users and across seasons — and report the LLM row of the benchmark table with an explicit footnote that it is measured on a subsample. An honest footnote beats a fake full-set number, and the subsampling argument is itself a finding about deployment cost.
10.3 Pros
The only model family in the plan that can work for genuinely cold users with no history at all, which is 30.14% of our test set.
Directly answers the cost/latency question that CLAUDE.md §8 requires and that a business audience actually cares about.
High presentation value for the final demo and slides.
A reference implementation exists on the same framework we are already using [7].
10.4 Cons
No item text natively — requires the profile-synthesis work above before any experiment is meaningful.
Documented position and popularity biases that require mitigation (candidate shuffling, repeated calls) which multiply cost [7].
Cost and latency are orders of magnitude worse than every other method here [21], so it can only ever be a reranker over a small top-N, never a retrieval stage.
Reproducibility is weaker than for any other family: API models change under you, and temperature must be pinned to zero with the model version recorded.
Non-trivial chance the honest result is "no improvement over the first stage" — which is a legitimate finding but a disappointing slide.

11. Methods deliberately excluded, and why
The mentor asked for the latest methods. Several of the genuinely latest lines of work are wrong for this project, and saying so with reasons is more useful than listing them as future work.

Semantic IDs exist to compress a large catalogue into decodable tokens. With 100 items, the item vocabulary is already smaller than a typical semantic-ID codebook. The method would add machinery and remove nothing.

13. The reproducibility literature and what it forces our protocol to do
This is the part of the research that most directly changes how we work, rather than what we build. The recommender-systems field has an unusually well-documented reproducibility problem, and a benchmark report that ignores it will be judged against it.
Finding in the literature
Protocol consequence for us
Neural recommenders often fail to beat tuned simple baselines [8], and the pattern has recurred for successive model generations including diffusion models [8b].
Strong baselines are not optional and must get the same tuning budget as the neural models. Our destination-conditioned popularity line is the real bar.
Model rankings flip with embedding size — GRU4Rec leads at 50 dimensions, SASRec at 256+ [3].
Report at least two embedding sizes for the sequence models, or state the single size used and flag the dependency as a limitation.
Framework implementations of the same model score differently [3].
Name the library and version for every result row. Our numbers are claims about a specific implementation.
BERT4Rec does not reproduce from its published configuration [9].
Exclude BERT4Rec (see §11).
Session-kNN beats GRU4Rec on many datasets [10].
Put a session-kNN baseline in the sequence-model comparison table.
Datasets vary in how much sequential structure they actually contain [11].
Measure the sequential structure of our booking sequences before interpreting the sequence-model results.
Preprocessing and split choices drive outcomes as much as architecture [3].
Freeze the split and the preprocessing in docs/eval_protocol.md before the first model is trained, and never change them mid-benchmark.
Personal insight — Write docs/eval_protocol.md as the very first Phase-2 deliverable, before any model code, and cite these papers inside it as the justification for each rule. It takes half a day and it is the single highest-return document in the project: it converts "we followed a protocol" from an assertion into a citation-backed design, and it stops the protocol quietly drifting to flatter whichever model is being worked on that week.

16. Shortlist: the four methodologies to carry into Phase 2
Selection rule: each candidate is scored on feasibility within a 2–3 week budget and on relevance to the data regime established in §2 and §3 — a 100-item catalogue, a median of two bookings per user, destination-dominant context, 30.14% cold test users, and a steep growth curve across the split. Coverage of the mentor's five-family plan is a constraint, not a tie-breaker. Lines of work that are obsolete or mis-sized for this regime (§11) are discarded outright. The non-personalised baseline tier — the popularity back-off chain, repeat-last, ItemKNN and EASE — is the mandatory floor required by the protocol and sits outside the count.

Highest relevance: context is the dominant signal (§3.2–3.3) and this family uses all of it. Highest feasibility: CPU, one library, no fallback policy needed for cold users (§3.6). Feature-group ablation for RQ1 comes free. Strongest recent competition precedent [15].
2
Sequential neural model
GRU4Rec via RecBole on booking-history sequences (primary) and on inferred click-then-book sessions (secondary, caveated per §3.8); SASRec as a second configuration only if the pipeline is stable by day 10; reversed-sequence augmentation and an SMF scoring head (§9.6) as optional ablations
3 d
Mandated by the phase plan and the only way to answer the sequence half of RQ1. GRU4Rec chosen over SASRec as primary because it is stronger at small embedding sizes and on short sequences [3], cheaper to train, and the same framework serves both. Evaluated on the warm slice (§3.6).

1–1.5 d

LLM reranking over synthesised cluster profiles
Deterministic text profile per cluster from training statistics; rerank the top-20 from rank 1 on a stratified test subsample; three-way ablation (full profile / destination-stripped / bare IDs); cost and latency logged per request
2–3 d
The only family with a story for the 30.14% cold test users (§3.6). Mandated for Phase 4. Answers the cost/latency question the business audience will ask. Feasible only because it is subsampled and reranks a fixed top-N; the profile design (§10.2) is what makes it honest on anonymous items.
Combined effort for the four: roughly 8.5–10.5 person-days, plus about 3.5 days for protocol, data pass, harness and baselines. That fits a three-week budget with time left for analysis and writing (§17).

On booking sequences with median length 2 it collapses to a smoothed ItemKNN and adds nothing (§7). On click sessions it is the cheapest way to test whether the 92% of discarded rows carry signal — but that experiment now depends on the session-inference decision in §3.8.

Run FM only if the RecBole configuration makes it near-free; DeepFM not planned
Rank 1 already represents the context-aware family with lower engineering risk and native cold-start handling; the literature does not show a decisive gain for the deep variants on typical tabular context [17].
Session-kNN (VSKNN)

The reproducibility literature says a sequence-model table without a session-kNN row is incomplete [10]; it costs little once sessions are defined.
Transformers4Rec (context-aware sequence model)
Stretch only
The right tool for injecting trip context into a sequence model, but the Merlin dependency chain is a schedule risk and the warm-slice ceiling (§3.6) limits what it can show.
Recency-weighted popularity
Promote into the baseline tier
A one-line extension motivated directly by the growth curve in §3.4; if it beats unweighted popularity, every learned model needs a recency feature.
16.3 Discarded as obsolete or mis-sized for this regime
Generative recommendation with semantic IDs (TIGER line), HSTU-style generative retrieval, diffusion recommenders, GNN session models, universal user representations, and BERT4Rec — for the reasons given in §11. In one sentence: they solve catalogue scale, throughput, or replication problems that a 100-item, two-bookings-per-user dataset does not have.
Personal insight — The four survivors form a deliberate spectrum: rank 1 uses all the context and none of the sequence, rank 2 uses the sequence and (in its basic form) none of the context, rank 3 uses neither, and rank 4 reasons over a text rendering of the context for the users the others cannot serve. Read together, the four results answer RQ1 by construction — whichever wins tells you which kind of information matters on this data — rather than by a single number. That is a more defensible structure for a benchmark than a longer list of models that overlap in what they consume.
17. Proposed 2–3 week implementation plan
The plan implements the shortlist in §16 in priority order, with the baseline floor first.
Week 1 — Protocol, data, and the honest floor
Day 1: Write docs/eval_protocol.md. Freeze split (validation = July–September 2014, test = after 2014-09-29 12:14:32.6, per §3.4), candidate set, metrics, K values, cold/light/warm user slices (§3.6), cold-start fallback policy, session-inference rule for click data (§3.8), tuning budget, seed policy. Cite the reproducibility literature as justification for each rule.
Day 2: One chunked pass over train.csv producing the processed Parquet tables: bookings with context, click-augmented session sequences, and the cluster-statistics table (which the LLM profiles will later need). Never read the 4.07 GB CSV again after this.
Day 3: Evaluation harness plus unit tests for every metric against small hand-computed examples, as CLAUDE.md §9 requires. Define the common ranked-top-K output format that every model will emit.
Day 4: Popularity back-off chain (including the recency-weighted variant from §3.4), repeat-last, ItemKNN, EASE. First rows in reports/benchmark_results.csv. Verify that the destination-conditioned popularity number reproduces the 53.07% probe.
Day 5: iALS, BPR-MF, item2vec. Beyond-accuracy metrics added to the harness. First full benchmark table exists at the end of week 1.
Week 2 — The two strongest contenders
Day 6: LightGBM multiclass context model with the full trip-context feature set. Expect this to be the strongest entry so far.
Day 7: Context feature-group ablation: drop party, dates/season, package, destination, channel in turn. This is the RQ1 evidence and produces the report's most informative chart.
Days 8–9: Sequence pipeline in RecBole, GRU4Rec on user-history sequences and on click-augmented sessions. Run the sequential-structure check [11] on both definitions and record the result.
Day 10: SASRec on the same pipeline at two embedding sizes, per the Betello finding [3]. Hard time-box: if the pipeline is not producing numbers by end of day 10, cut SASRec and move on with GRU4Rec alone.
Week 3 — LLM, analysis, write-up
Day 11: Generate the 100 cluster profiles from training statistics with a deterministic template. Build the prompt and the rerank harness with cost and latency logging.
Day 12: Run the three-way ablation (full profile / destination-stripped profile / bare IDs) on the stratified subsample. Log everything.
Day 13: Seed repeats for the cheap models, per-slice analysis (cold vs warm users, by season, by party type, by destination), beyond-accuracy table.
Days 14–15: Write-up, charts, limitations section, and the "methods considered and excluded" table from §11.
Personal insight — Note the shape of this plan: models occupy roughly six of fifteen days. The rest is protocol, data, harness and analysis. That ratio is correct for a benchmark report and is the opposite of the instinct to start training on day one. The single most common way a project like this fails is producing many trained models and no trustworthy comparison between them.

19. Limitations to state explicitly in the final report
hotel_cluster is a proxy for room category or package, not the thing itself, and it is anonymous. Every business conclusion inherits that proxy.
The Kaggle competition for this dataset was decided by a data leak; our numbers are not comparable to its leaderboard, and published figures on this dataset may or may not have excluded the leak (§4).
Calendar seasonality is nearly invisible at the cluster level globally (JS divergence 0.0019 bit for check-in month); any seasonal claim must come from the per-destination slice.
With a median of two bookings per user, results for sequence models are statements about a sparse-history regime and should not be generalised to dense-history domains.
Framework implementation choices affect model rankings [3]; our results name a specific library and version.
The LLM reranking row is measured on a stratified subsample, not the full test set, for cost reasons that are themselves a reported result.
Cluster profiles for the LLM are synthesised from training statistics by a deterministic template; they are derived data, clearly labelled, not source data.
Catalogue coverage is near-trivial with 100 items; intra-list diversity and popularity bias are the meaningful beyond-accuracy metrics, sliced per destination and per season.
Expedia is under Kaggle competition terms: research use, no redistribution of raw data.
In-stay services (spa, entertainment, food and beverage) have no public dataset and are a stated gap, not a modelled result.
